How to Scan Sheet Music with Your Phone

By Scanner.cam Editorial Team · Updated 12 Nov 2026 · 5 min read

Sheet music is awkward to carry, easy to lose, and expensive to replace — and a folder of loose pages is no way to practise. A clean scan turns a paper score into a file you can read on a tablet, mark up with a stylus, share with your teacher or ensemble, and keep forever. The trick is capturing the staves straight and sharp, because a single warped line makes a score hard to read at tempo.

What you need

  • Your phone. Any modern phone camera is sharp enough for a page of sheet music.
  • Bright, even light. Daylight near a window is ideal. Avoid a single lamp, which creates a bright spot and dark corners on the page.
  • A flat surface. A table or music stand laid flat. Heavy books or small weights hold down pages that want to spring shut.
  • A free scanner. Scanner.cam runs in your browser — no app, no account — and corrects the page's perspective automatically.

Scan sheet music in 5 steps

  1. Flatten the page. For a bound score, open it flat and weigh down the edges. For a single sheet, press out any curl with your palm first — a curved page bends the staves.
  2. Place it in even light. Put the page on a plain, dark surface if the paper is white: the contrast helps the scanner find the page edges. Keep your own shadow out of the frame.
  3. Hold the phone directly above the page. Shoot straight down, not at an angle. An angled shot makes the staves converge, and the music becomes hard to follow. Fill the frame with the page, leaving only a small margin.
  4. Capture and zoom in. Check the smallest notes, the key signature, and any dynamic markings. If a bar is blurry or a staff line is cut off, retake it — a score you cannot read at sight is useless in rehearsal.
  5. Save as one PDF per piece. Combine all pages of a piece into a single multi-page PDF, named by composer and title — bach-minuet-in-g.pdf. One file per piece keeps your library tidy and makes page turns easy on a tablet.

Keep the staves straight

Sheet music is unforgiving about geometry: a page photographed at a slight angle turns parallel staves into converging lines. Three habits keep your scans readable:

  • Shoot from directly above. Line the phone up so the page edges run parallel to the screen edges. Most scanner apps show edge guides — use them.
  • Let the scanner straighten the page. Scanner.cam detects the page corners and corrects perspective, but it can only do so much with a badly angled photo. A straight shot in, a straight page out.
  • Watch for page curl near the spine. Bound scores curl near the centre, bending the inner staves. Press the book flat or scan one page at a time with the spine held open.

Scan multi-page pieces without losing the order

A piece that runs to four or eight pages needs a system, or the pages end up out of order:

  • Scan in order, top to bottom. Work through the piece page by page and add each page to the same PDF as you go.
  • Number the pages in the file. If your scanner numbers pages automatically, check the order before saving. A misplaced page in the middle of a piece is easy to miss until you are playing it.
  • Check page turns. On a tablet, a multi-page PDF turns with a tap — but only if the pages are in the right order and the right way up. Flick through the whole file once before rehearsal.

Reading your scans on a tablet

A tablet is the best way to practise from a scan: it sits on a music stand, turns pages with a tap, and never blows away. A few tips make it work well:

  • Export at full resolution. Small notes and articulation marks need every pixel. Do not downscale the scan to save space — a single piece is only a few megabytes.
  • Use a PDF reader with annotation. Mark fingerings, breath marks, and reminders directly on the scan with a stylus, and keep the original paper copy untouched.
  • Keep a backup. Copy your music library to cloud storage. A lost tablet should never mean a lost library.

Frequently asked questions

Can I scan sheet music with my phone camera?
Yes. Lay the page flat in bright, even light, photograph it straight from above, and save it as a PDF with a free browser scanner like Scanner.cam. Check the smallest notes and the key signature at full zoom before you finish.

What file format is best for scanned sheet music?
PDF, one file per piece. PDFs keep the pages in order, print cleanly, and open on any tablet or computer. Keep each piece as a single multi-page PDF named by composer and title.

How do I scan a whole music book?
Work through it page by page in good light, adding each page to one multi-page PDF, or scan one piece at a time as its own file. Weigh the book open and press curled pages flat first — the spine is where most scans go wrong.

Will scanning damage old or fragile sheet music?
No. Phone scanning is contact-free, so nothing touches the page. It is safer than a flatbed scanner for old, brittle, or annotated scores, and the digital copy protects you if the original is lost.

Can I share scanned sheet music with my teacher or ensemble?
You can share scans of music you own or that is in the public domain. Do not scan and distribute copyrighted scores you do not have the rights to share — buy the music, or use a licensed digital edition, and share only your own annotations and practice notes.
