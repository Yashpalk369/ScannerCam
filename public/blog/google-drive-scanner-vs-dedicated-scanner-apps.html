Google Drive Scanner vs Dedicated Scanner Apps: Which Should You Use?

By Scanner.cam Team · Updated 27 Oct 2026 · 6 min read
Quick answer

Almost everyone already has Google Drive on their phone, and Drive has a capable document scanner built in. So why do dedicated scanner apps exist at all?

The answer is that Drive's scanner is excellent at one job — getting a paper document into your Drive as a searchable Portable Document Format (PDF) file — while dedicated apps cover a wider set of jobs: text extraction, ID modes, signing, and offline work. This guide compares them feature by feature so you can pick the right tool for how you actually scan.

The short answer

If you already live in Google Drive and mostly need to file receipts, forms, and multi-page documents where you can find them again, Drive's built-in scanner is enough — it is free, it saves straight to your Drive, and its scans are searchable. If you need OCR (optical character recognition) text export, ID-card or book modes, e-signatures, annotation, or scanning without a Google account, a dedicated scanner app — or a browser-based scanner — is the better pick. The two are not rivals so much as different defaults: Drive optimizes for storage, dedicated apps optimize for the scan itself.

What Google Drive's scanner does

Drive's scanner is available in the Google Drive app on Android and iOS — it arrived on iPhone in late 2023 after nearly a decade as an Android-only feature. The web version of Drive cannot scan; you need the mobile app and a Google account. The workflow is simple:

  1. Open the Drive app and tap the camera button (Android) or the Scan option (iPhone).
  2. Point the camera at the page. Auto capture snaps the shot when it detects the page; manual mode lets you press the shutter yourself.
  3. Adjust the result with crop and rotate, filters including grayscale, and a Clean tool that removes stains, fingers, and similar marks.
  4. Add more pages, then save as a PDF or JPG (Joint Photographic Experts Group) file into the Drive folder of your choice, with a title Drive suggests for you.

On some newer Android phones, Drive offers an enhanced document scanner that can spot and separate several pages in a single camera view — handy for a spread of receipts. Drive also makes saved scans searchable, so text inside your PDFs can be found from Drive search. For filing and retrieval, that combination — scan, save, search — is genuinely strong, and it costs nothing beyond the Google account you already have.

What dedicated scanner apps add

Dedicated apps — CamScanner, Adobe Scan, Microsoft Lens, Apple Notes, and browser-based scanners like Scanner.cam — compete on the scanning experience itself. Depending on the app, you get:

  • OCR text export: convert a scan into editable text, Word, or Excel files. Drive makes scans searchable, but it does not hand you the extracted text to edit.
  • ID and book modes: dedicated layouts for identity cards (front and back on one page) and for book pages (flattening curved pages, splitting spreads).
  • E-signatures and annotation: sign, highlight, and mark up scans inside the app.
  • Batch and auto-capture modes: rapid multi-page capture, business-card modes, and automatic page separation.
  • No-account scanning: browser-based scanners work with no install and no account at all, and process everything on your device rather than uploading to a cloud.
  • Advanced cleanup: shadow removal, perspective correction tuning, and filter sets tuned for documents rather than photos.

The trade is that dedicated apps usually come with their own costs: an install, often an account, sometimes ads or a watermark on the free tier, and a subscription for the full feature set. CamScanner's free tier, for example, watermarks exports and reserves OCR, ID mode, and e-signatures for its Premium subscription.

Head-to-head

  • Cost: both free at the basic level. Drive's scanner has no watermark and no paid tier for scanning; dedicated apps range from completely free (browser-based) to freemium with subscriptions.
  • Account required: Drive requires a Google account and saves to your Drive cloud. Browser-based scanners require nothing; installed apps usually want an account for sync.
  • Where scans live: Drive uploads your scans to Google's cloud — convenient for access anywhere, but a copy exists outside your device. Device-local tools keep scans on your phone only.
  • Scan quality basics: comparable. Both do edge detection, perspective correction, filters, and multi-page PDF export well.
  • OCR and editing: dedicated apps win clearly — text export, ID/book modes, signing, and annotation are their home turf.
  • Finding scans later: Drive wins for Drive users — scans land in your existing folders and are searchable. Dedicated apps need their own organization or an export step to Drive.
  • Offline use: Drive's scanner needs the app and ultimately uploads to your Drive; a browser-based or device-local scanner works without an account and without sending anything anywhere.
  • Platform: Drive's scanner is mobile-app only — no scanning from the Drive website. Browser-based scanners work on any device with a browser, including desktops and Chromebooks.

Which should you use?

  • Use Google Drive's scanner if: you already organize your life in Drive, you want scans filed and searchable with zero extra apps, and you are comfortable with a Google account and cloud storage.
  • Use a dedicated scanner app if: you need OCR text export, ID-card or book modes, e-signatures, or annotation — or you scan daily and want a workflow built around scanning rather than filing.
  • Use a browser-based scanner if: you want no install, no account, and no upload — for example on a borrowed phone, a shared computer, or for sensitive documents you would rather never send to a cloud.
  • Use more than one if: your needs split. Many people file everyday paperwork with Drive and keep a browser scanner bookmarked for quick, private, one-off scans. There is no rule that says one tool must do everything.

The real question is not which scanner is "best" in the abstract — it is what happens to the scan after you take it. If the answer is "it goes into my Drive and I search for it later," Drive's scanner is already the right tool and already installed. If the answer is "I need editable text, a signature, or a clean file with no account attached," that is exactly the gap dedicated scanners exist to fill.

Can I scan documents with Google Drive?
Yes. The Google Drive app on Android and iOS has a built-in scanner: open the app, tap the camera button (Android) or the Scan option (iPhone), capture pages, adjust crop and filters, and save as a PDF or JPG file in the Drive folder you choose. The Drive website cannot scan — you need the mobile app and a Google account.

Is Google Drive's scanner free?
Yes. Scanning in the Drive app is free, with no watermark on exports. Your scans count against your Google storage quota like any other Drive file.

Does Google Drive's scanner do OCR?
Drive makes saved scans searchable, so text inside your PDFs can be found from Drive search. But it does not export extracted text into editable Word or Excel files the way dedicated scanner apps with full OCR do.

Can I scan without a Google account?
Not with Drive's scanner — it requires a Google account and saves to your Drive. For account-free scanning, use a browser-based scanner, which needs no install and no account.

Is a dedicated scanner app better than Google Drive?
For OCR text export, ID and book modes, e-signatures, and annotation, yes. For filing scans into Drive and searching them later, Drive's own scanner is simpler and already installed. Pick by what happens to the scan after you take it.
