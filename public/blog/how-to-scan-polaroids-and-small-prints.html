How to Scan Polaroids and Small Prints

By Scanner.cam Editorial Team · Updated 8 Nov 2026 · 5 min read
Quick answer

Scan Polaroids and small prints by placing them flat on a dark, matte surface in bright, even light — and never using flash, which bounces straight off the glossy surface. A phone camera held close and steady captures the whole print, and a quick crop and straighten turns it into a clean digital copy.

Small prints are the hardest photos to scan well, and Polaroids are the hardest of all. They are tiny, so every flaw is magnified when you enlarge them; they are glossy, so any direct light becomes a white hotspot; and Polaroids have that thick white border that confuses edge detection. None of that needs special equipment — it needs the right setup and a steady hand. If you are working through a whole album of prints, the family photo album guide covers the project plan; this guide is the capture technique for the small stuff.

Why small prints are tricky

A 4×6 print scanned at phone resolution gives you plenty of pixels to work with. A wallet print or a Polaroid gives you far fewer, so a slightly soft shot becomes a very soft enlargement. Gloss makes it worse: the shiny coating reflects the light source straight back at the camera, washing out the middle of the image. And Polaroid film has a thick border and a chemical pod at the bottom that make the print thicker than normal paper, so it never lies perfectly flat. The fix for all three problems is the same: flat, even, indirect light, and a close, steady, straight-down shot.

Set up a glare-free shot

  1. Work on a dark, matte surface. Black cloth or dark card. It kills reflections, makes the white Polaroid border easy to detect, and keeps the background out of the final crop.
  2. Light from the side, from two directions if you can. A window on one side and a desk lamp on the other evens out the light across the glossy surface. Never light from behind the camera — that is where the glare comes from.
  3. Turn the flash off and keep it off. Flash on a glossy print is a white blob in the middle of the photo, every time. If the room is dim, add a lamp instead of using flash.
  4. Angle the print slightly if glare persists. Tilting the print a few degrees moves the reflection off the image area. Keep the phone square to the print and tilt the print, not the camera.
  5. Wipe the print first. A soft, dry cloth removes dust and fingerprints that show up ten times larger in the scan. Never use water or cleaning spray on a Polaroid.

Capture close and steady

  1. Fill the frame with the print. The closer the shot, the more pixels the photo gets. Leave just a small margin around the edges for the crop.
  2. Hold the phone parallel to the print. A tilted shot turns the rectangle into a trapezoid and softens the far edge. Rest your elbows on the table or use a small tripod.
  3. Tap to focus on the middle of the image. Phone cameras focus where you tap — tap the centre of the photo, not the border, and wait half a second for the focus to settle.
  4. Check at full zoom. Pinch-zoom the first shot: faces should be sharp, not mushy. If they are soft, add light and hold steadier rather than moving closer.
  5. Shoot the back when it has writing. Polaroids often have names and dates on the back in pen. Photograph the back and keep it with the front file.

Batch small prints without losing track

One print at a time is slow when you have fifty. Lay four to six prints in a neat grid on the dark cloth, leaving a gap between them, and photograph the whole grid in one shot — then crop each print out afterwards. Keep the grid in the same order every time and name the files as you crop, so nothing gets mixed up. For very small prints, two per shot is the practical limit: any more and each print gets too few pixels. And if you plan to enlarge a wallet print to a full-size copy, check the best DPI for scanning old photos first — small originals need the highest resolution you can give them.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using flash. The single worst thing you can do to a glossy print.
  • Shooting from too far away. A small print shot from arm's length has too few pixels to enlarge. Get close.
  • Forgetting the border. The white Polaroid border is part of the photo — crop to the outside of it, not the inside.
  • Cleaning with water or spray. Polaroid chemistry and water do not mix. Dry cloth only.
  • Stacking prints to save time. Prints stick together and scratch each other. One layer, one shot.

Frequently asked questions

How do I scan a Polaroid without glare?
Lay it flat on a dark, matte surface in bright side light, with the flash off. If glare persists, tilt the print a few degrees so the reflection moves off the image — keep the phone square to the print.

Can I scan Polaroids with my phone?
Yes. A phone camera held close, steady, and parallel to the print captures a Polaroid well. Fill the frame, tap to focus on the middle, and check the first shot at full zoom.

Should I include the white Polaroid border in the scan?
Yes — the border is part of the photo. Crop to the outside edge of the border, not the inside, and keep any writing on the back as a second shot.

How do I scan lots of small prints quickly?
Lay four to six prints in a grid on a dark cloth with gaps between them, photograph the whole grid, then crop each print out. For very small prints, two per shot is the practical limit.

What resolution do I need for small prints?
The highest you can get: small originals need more resolution, not less, when you enlarge them. Capture at your phone's full resolution and see the DPI guide for the numbers.
