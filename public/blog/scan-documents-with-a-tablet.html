Can I Scan Documents With a Tablet?

By Scanner.cam Team · Updated 21 Oct 2026 · 7 min read
Quick answer

Yes — a tablet scans documents just as well as a phone, and for some jobs it's better. An iPad, an Android tablet, or a Surface tablet all have a rear camera, a big screen for reviewing pages, and free scanning options built in or one browser tab away.

The real question isn't whether your tablet can scan; it's which tablet scans best for the way you work. This guide compares iPad, Android, and Surface/Windows tablet scanning head to head so you can pick the right method instead of guessing.

If you already know your tablet and just want steps, we have those too: our iPad scanning walkthrough, our Android tablet guide, and our Surface tablet guide each go method by method. What follows is the decision guide — strengths, weaknesses, and the method I'd hand to a friend for each use case.

The short version: which tablet should you scan with?

  • Best overall experience: iPad. The Files and Notes apps both scan well, auto-crop is reliable, and the large screen makes reviewing multi-page documents genuinely pleasant.
  • Best free-and-flexible: Android tablet. Google Drive's built-in scanner is excellent and free, and you can combine it with any browser-based tool without an account.
  • Best for office workflows: Surface / Windows tablet. The Windows Camera app scans straight to your PC environment, and you can drop pages directly into documents you're editing.
  • Best universal method (any tablet): the browser. Open Scanner.cam on any tablet and scan with no install, no account, and no watermark — the same workflow on iPad, Android, and Surface.

iPad scanning: what you're working with

Every modern iPad carries two good scanning paths out of the box. The Notes app can scan documents directly into a note — open a note, tap the camera icon, choose Scan Documents, and the iPad auto-detects page edges, straightens, and saves. The Files app does the same but saves straight to iCloud Drive or On My iPad, which is the better choice when the scan needs to end up somewhere specific. Both handle multi-page documents by capturing page after page into one PDF.

Where the iPad shines is review: on a 10- or 11-inch screen you can actually read the captured text before saving, which catches skewed or blurry pages a phone screen hides. Where it lags: older iPads have noticeably worse rear cameras than current iPhones, so fine print can come out softer than the same scan taken on a phone. And everything Apple touches wants to live in Apple's ecosystem — getting a PDF from Files to a non-Apple destination takes an extra share-sheet hop.

Android tablet scanning: what you're working with

Android tablets are less uniform, but the star is consistent: Google Drive's built-in scanner. Tap the + (New) button in the Drive app, choose Scan (or the camera icon on newer versions), and you get auto-crop, filters, and multi-page PDFs saved straight to Drive — free, with no extra app. Samsung tablets add their own camera-based document detection on top. Because Android is Android, the exact steps vary by brand and Android version, but Drive works the same everywhere.

The Android advantage is flexibility: Drive is everywhere, sharing to non-Google destinations is straightforward, and cheap Android tablets with decent cameras are plentiful. The catch is inconsistency — a 2021 budget tablet's camera and an old Android build will scan noticeably worse than a current Galaxy Tab, and some vendors bury the scan feature in odd menu locations.

Surface and Windows tablets: what you're working with

Surface tablets (and other Windows 2-in-1s) scan through the Windows Camera app's Document mode, which detects page edges and exports scans you can save, print, or drop into Word and OneNote. The killer feature here is context: your scanned pages land in the same environment where the report, the email, or the form lives. No transfer step, no share sheet, no cable.

The trade-off is camera quality. Tablet rear cameras on Windows devices are functional but rarely great, and Document mode is simpler than what phone scanner apps offer — fewer filters, less aggressive perspective correction. For office paperwork it's plenty; for fine print, check the result at 100% zoom before you file it.

Head-to-head comparison

Here's how the three families stack up on the things that actually matter for scanning:

  • Camera quality: iPad ≈ Android flagship > Surface ≈ budget Android. For fine print, the tablet with the better camera wins regardless of brand.
  • Built-in scanning: All three have it — Notes/Files on iPad, Drive on Android, Camera app on Windows. None costs anything.
  • Multi-page PDFs: All three handle it natively. The iPad's big-screen review is the most pleasant; Drive's is the most cloud-convenient.
  • Ease of sharing: Android shares to anything with the least friction. iPad is smoothest inside Apple apps. Surface wins when the destination is a Windows document you're already editing.
  • Screen for review: Any tablet beats any phone here — and this is the underrated reason to scan on a tablet at all. Catching a bad page on a 10-inch screen takes two seconds; discovering it after you've emailed the PDF costs you the whole job.
  • Watermarks and costs: None of the built-in methods add watermarks or charge you. (Third-party scanner apps may — stick to the built-ins or a free web tool.)

The browser method: identical on every tablet

If you don't want to learn three different built-in workflows — or your tablet is a hand-me-down whose apps are outdated — the browser method is the same everywhere: open Scanner.cam, allow camera access, capture pages, and export a PDF. This is also the method to reach for on a shared or borrowed tablet, where you don't want to sign into anyone's cloud account just to scan a page.

One practical note: tablets' rear cameras sit off-center, so hold the tablet level and let the page fill the frame rather than hovering at an angle. And wipe the lens — tablets live in bags and on sofas, and a dusty lens is the most common cause of "my scans look hazy."

Which method for which job

  • Expense receipts on the road: whatever tablet you carry — Drive on Android, Files on iPad. Speed matters more than polish.
  • Multi-page contracts to sign and return: iPad (best review screen) or Surface (lands straight into your Windows workflow).
  • Homework and study notes: the browser method — fastest, works on a school-issued locked-down tablet where you can't install apps.
  • IDs and sensitive documents: a method that processes on-device — the browser route with a tool like Scanner.cam, or the built-in app with cloud sync paused. Never let a random third-party scanner app upload your ID to its servers.
  • Whiteboards and flip charts: any tablet — the big screen is a genuine advantage for framing a whole board. (We have a dedicated whiteboard scanning guide too.)

FAQ

Can I scan documents with a tablet?
Yes. iPads scan through the Notes or Files app, Android tablets through Google Drive's built-in scanner, and Surface tablets through the Windows Camera app's Document mode — all free, all without extra apps. Or use a browser-based tool like Scanner.cam, which works identically on every tablet.

Which is better for scanning: iPad or Android tablet?
For most people it's a tie decided by camera quality and ecosystem. iPads offer the smoothest built-in experience and the best review screen; Android tablets offer Google Drive's excellent free scanner and easier sharing to non-Google destinations. Pick the tablet you already own — don't buy one just to scan.

Can a Surface tablet scan documents?
Yes. The Windows Camera app includes a Document mode that detects page edges and exports scans for use in Word, OneNote, or email. It's the most convenient option when the scanned pages need to end up in a Windows workflow.

Do I need to install an app to scan on a tablet?
No. Every major tablet OS includes scanning out of the box, and browser-based scanners like Scanner.cam need nothing installed at all — handy on shared, school, or work tablets where installs are blocked.

Is scanning on a tablet better than on a phone?
For reviewing pages, yes — the big screen makes it much easier to catch blurry or skewed pages before you save. For raw camera quality, a flagship phone usually wins. For multi-page documents, the tablet's review advantage often matters more than the phone's camera advantage.
