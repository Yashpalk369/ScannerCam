What's Better for Receipts: A Scanner App or a Spreadsheet Photo?

By Scanner.cam Team · Updated 29 Oct 2026 · 5 min read
Quick answer

For receipts, a scanner app is better than a plain photo dropped into a spreadsheet in almost every case that matters. A scanner straightens, crops and cleans the receipt into a readable PDF, while a spreadsheet photo is usually a tilted, shadowed picture that is hard to read months later.

Use a photo only for a quick, throwaway note; use a scan whenever the receipt may be claimed, audited or kept as proof.

What a scanner app actually gives you

A scanner app does more than take a picture. It finds the edges of the receipt, corrects the perspective so the paper looks flat, boosts contrast so faded print stays readable, and saves the result as a proper PDF. Long receipts can be captured as one continuous document, and several receipts can be combined into a single expense file. That matters because a receipt is not a memory aid — it is evidence. A finance team, a client or a tax preparer needs to read the vendor, date, items and total without squinting at a crooked photo.

Browser-based scanners such as Scanner.cam add one more advantage: nothing needs installing, there is no account to create, and the scan is processed on your device. You open the scanner, capture the receipt, and download a clean PDF that you can then attach to an expense claim or store wherever you keep records.

What a spreadsheet photo gives you

Photographing a receipt and pasting it into a spreadsheet is fast, and that speed is its only real advantage. The photo sits next to the amount you typed in, so at a glance you can see what the row refers to. For a personal note — "this was the taxi on Tuesday" — that is often enough.

The problems appear later. Spreadsheet photos are rarely cropped or straightened, so text near the edges gets cut off and shadows from your hand darken the total. The image is usually a single JPEG with no searchable text, so you cannot search for a vendor name across a year of expenses. And when you share the spreadsheet, you share a bulky file full of uncompressed photos that is awkward to print, forward or file. Worst of all, if you ever need to replace the photo with a proper scan, the original paper receipt is often long gone.

Side by side: scanner app vs spreadsheet photo

  • Readability: scanner wins — perspective correction and contrast make faded thermal receipts legible. A raw photo keeps every shadow and angle.
  • Proof value: scanner wins — a clean, single PDF per receipt (or per claim) is what expense systems expect. A photo inside a spreadsheet cell is not a document.
  • Search and filing: scanner wins — named PDFs in dated folders can be found in seconds. Photos buried in spreadsheet rows cannot be searched by vendor or date.
  • Speed: spreadsheet photo wins for a single throwaway note — one photo, one paste, done.
  • Sharing: scanner wins — a PDF attaches cleanly to an email or expense form, while a photo-heavy spreadsheet is bulky and prints badly.
  • Long receipts: scanner wins — a scanner can capture a long till receipt as one document; a single photo often cuts off the bottom half.

When a spreadsheet photo is enough

A photo is fine when the receipt has no claim attached to it: you are tracking personal spending for yourself, you only need a reminder of what you bought, and you will delete it within days. Even then, take the photo straight-on, in good light, with the whole receipt in frame — and type the amount and vendor into the spreadsheet row at the same time, because the photo alone will not remind you of either in six months.

When a scanner app is the right choice

Use a scan whenever money may change hands on the strength of the receipt: expense claims, client billing, tax records, warranty claims, and business purchases. In those cases the scan is the record, and the spreadsheet is just the summary. Scan first, then enter the totals in your spreadsheet and link or attach the PDF. That order — scan, then summarise — is the habit that saves expense reports.

A simple receipt workflow that works

  1. Scan each receipt the day you get it, before thermal print fades or the paper is lost. Flat surface, good light, whole receipt in frame.
  2. Name the file the same way every time, for example 2026-10-29-taxi-airport.pdf — date first, so files sort themselves.
  3. Save scans in one folder per month or per trip, not scattered across your camera roll.
  4. Enter the date, vendor and total in your spreadsheet, and add the PDF filename or a link to the file in the same row.
  5. Check the scan before you throw the paper away: vendor, date and total must all be readable. If any line is cut off, rescan while you still have the receipt.

That workflow gives you both worlds: a spreadsheet you can total and filter, backed by clean PDFs you can actually hand to someone. The photo-only shortcut skips the part that matters most — the readable record.

Is a photo of a receipt good enough for expenses?
Sometimes, for a personal note. For an expense claim, a proper scan is safer: it is cropped, straightened and readable, and it saves as a PDF that expense systems and finance teams can accept and file.

Should I scan receipts or photograph them for a spreadsheet?
Scan them first, then summarise in the spreadsheet. The scan is the proof; the spreadsheet row is the summary. A photo pasted into a spreadsheet is hard to read, search and share later.

How should I name scanned receipts?
Use a consistent pattern with the date first, for example 2026-10-29-taxi-airport.pdf, and keep one folder per month or trip. Date-first names sort chronologically without any extra effort.

Do I need an app to scan receipts?
No. A browser-based scanner like Scanner.cam scans to PDF with no install and no signup, and the finished PDF can be attached to an expense claim or saved wherever you keep records.

What if a receipt is faded or very long?
Scan it as soon as possible, since thermal print fades over time. Capture a long receipt in sections if needed and combine the pages into one PDF, then check that the vendor, date and total are all readable before discarding the paper.
