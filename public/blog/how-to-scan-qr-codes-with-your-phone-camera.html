How to Scan QR Codes With Your Phone Camera (No App)

By Scanner.cam Team · Updated 26 Oct 2026 · 6 min read
Quick answer

Almost every phone made in the last few years can read QR codes with the camera app that is already on it — no extra app, no account, no download. Point the camera at the code, tap the notification that pops up, and you are on your way.

This guide shows you how it works on iPhone and Android, what to do when a code will not scan, and how to stay safe before you tap an unknown link.

A quick distinction first: scanning a QR code is not the same as scanning a document. A QR code is a machine-readable shortcut — your phone decodes it into a web address, a Wi-Fi password, or a payment prompt. A document scan produces a picture or PDF of a page. This guide covers the QR side; if you need a document as a PDF, scan it as a document instead.

On iPhone: use the Camera app

Every iPhone running iOS 11 or later reads QR codes straight from the Camera app:

  1. Open the Camera app and switch to Photo mode.
  2. Point the camera at the QR code so the whole code fits inside the frame. You do not need to take a photo.
  3. Wait for the yellow notification banner to appear at the top of the screen, showing where the code leads.
  4. Tap the banner to open the link, join the Wi-Fi network, or save the contact — whatever the code was made for.

If nothing appears, check Settings > Camera > Scan QR Codes is switched on. It is on by default, but it can be turned off, and that is the most common reason an iPhone "cannot" scan QR codes.

On Android: use the camera or Google Lens

Android varies by manufacturer, but you almost always have a built-in option:

  • Google Pixel: open the Camera app and point it at the code — Pixel cameras detect QR codes automatically and show a tappable link.
  • Samsung Galaxy: open the Camera app, point it at the code, and tap the pop-up. If nothing appears, open Camera settings and make sure "Scan QR codes" is enabled.
  • Other Android phones: if your camera app does not react to QR codes, open Google Lens (in the Camera app, in the Google app, or as its own app), point it at the code, and tap the result.

No matter which route you use, the built-in camera or Lens is enough — you do not need a third-party QR scanner app, and many of those apps are ad-heavy or ask for permissions they do not need.

Scanning a QR code from a screenshot or photo

Sometimes the code is not in front of you — it is in a screenshot, a photo someone sent you, or an image on a web page. You can still read it without a special app:

  1. On iPhone: open the photo in the Photos app, long-press on the QR code, and tap the link that appears. On newer iOS versions, Live Text recognises QR codes inside images automatically.
  2. On Android: open the image in Google Photos, tap the Lens button, and Lens will decode the code and show the destination.
  3. On a computer: open the image, and use your phone's camera or Lens pointed at the screen — or drag the image into Google Lens in the browser.

If the code is small or blurry in the photo, zoom in on the image first so the code fills more of the screen, then let Lens or Live Text read it again.

When a QR code will not scan

  • Too far or too close: hold the phone about 15–30 cm from the code, then move slowly closer or further away until the banner appears.
  • Poor light or glare: move out of direct sunlight, tilt the phone slightly to kill reflections on screens and glossy print, or turn on the flash.
  • Dirty lens or damaged code: wipe the camera lens, and check whether the code itself is torn, faded, or partly covered — a damaged code may simply be unreadable.
  • Code too small: tiny codes on packaging or receipts need you to get close and hold steady; tap to focus on the code if your camera keeps focusing elsewhere.
  • QR scanning switched off: on iPhone check Settings > Camera > Scan QR Codes; on Samsung check Camera settings > Scan QR codes.

Stay safe before you tap

QR codes hide their destination until your phone decodes them, which is exactly what makes them useful for phishing — a technique sometimes called "quishing." A sticker placed over a legitimate code can send you to a fake login page. Protect yourself with one habit:

  • Read the preview before you tap. Your phone shows the web address in the banner. If it looks wrong — a misspelled brand, a strange domain, a URL shortener you did not expect — do not tap it.
  • Never enter passwords or payment details from a QR code you found in a public place unless you were expecting that exact page.
  • Be extra careful with codes on parking meters, posters, and restaurant tables — these are the spots where fake stickers turn up most often.
  • Do not scan codes in unsolicited messages promising prizes, refunds, or account warnings. Legitimate companies do not ask you to verify an account by scanning a code from a random message.

When the destination checks out, tap away. When it does not, ignore the code — a legitimate business will always offer another way to reach the same page.

What QR codes can do beyond web links

A QR code can carry more than a URL. Depending on how it was created, scanning one can:

  • Join a Wi-Fi network automatically, without typing the password.
  • Save a contact straight into your address book.
  • Open a payment or banking prompt with the amount pre-filled.
  • Show plain text such as a menu, a ticket number, or an event detail.
  • Open an app store page for a specific app.

Your phone handles each type the same way — scan, read the preview, then decide. The preview step is what keeps a convenience feature from becoming a security risk.

Do I need an app to scan QR codes?
No. iPhones read QR codes in the Camera app, and most Android phones do too — Pixel and Samsung cameras detect them automatically, and Google Lens covers the rest. Third-party QR scanner apps are unnecessary and often ad-heavy.

Why won't my phone scan a QR code?
The usual reasons are distance, glare, a dirty lens, a damaged code, or QR scanning switched off in camera settings. Move to 15–30 cm, kill reflections, wipe the lens, and check the camera setting before assuming the code is broken.

Can I scan a QR code from a screenshot?
Yes. On iPhone, long-press the code in the Photos app; on Android, open the image in Google Photos and tap Lens. Both decode QR codes inside saved images without a separate app.

Is it safe to scan random QR codes?
Read the destination preview before tapping. If the web address looks wrong or unexpected, do not tap it — fake stickers over real codes are a known phishing trick. Never enter passwords or payment details from a code you found in a public place unless you expected that exact page.

Can my phone scan a QR code and a document with the same camera?
Yes, but they are different jobs. QR scanning decodes a code into a link or action; document scanning produces a picture or PDF of a page. Use the camera's QR detection for codes and a document scanner — like Scanner.cam in your browser — for pages.
