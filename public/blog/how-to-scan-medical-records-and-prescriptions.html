How to Scan Medical Records and Prescriptions

By Scanner.cam Editorial Team · Updated 10 Nov 2026 · 4 min read
Quick answer

To scan medical records and prescriptions, capture each page in colour as a clear PDF, file it under the patient's name and the date of the visit, and keep the originals safe. A well-organised digital health file lets you hand a doctor your full history in seconds, instead of digging through a drawer during the appointment.

Medical paperwork accumulates fast: lab reports, prescriptions, discharge summaries, vaccination cards, insurance letters. Paper fades, folders get lost, and a report from two years ago is often the one a new doctor asks for. Scanning it all once, properly, turns a pile of paper into a file you can search, share, and back up.

Sort before you scan

Scanning is quick; finding a record later is the hard part. Spend ten minutes sorting first and the system builds itself:

  • Group by person, then by type: lab reports, prescriptions, imaging, discharge summaries, vaccination records, insurance.
  • Order each group by date, newest first or oldest first — pick one and stick to it.
  • Pull out duplicates and keep only the clearest copy of each document.
  • Set aside anything faded or damaged to scan first, while the text is still readable.

Scan each document properly

Medical documents are read for exact numbers — a dosage, a lab value, a date. Legibility is not optional.

  1. Scan in colour. Colour preserves stamps, handwritten notes, and highlighted values that black and white can wash out.
  2. Capture the whole page, including letterheads and the doctor's signature at the bottom. A cropped page looks incomplete and a doctor may not trust it.
  3. Check the numbers before moving on. Zoom in on lab values, dosages, and dates. If a figure is blurry, re-scan — a misread dosage is worse than no record at all.
  4. Scan both sides of every page. Prescriptions and reports often carry notes, stamps, or a second set of results on the back.
  5. Handle fragile paper gently. Old reports on thin paper tear easily; lay them flat and scan with the phone held steady rather than pressing them under glass.

Name and store files so you can find them

A medical file is only useful if you can find the right record in under a minute. Use a naming pattern that works without opening the file:

  • Start with the date: 2026-09-14-blood-test-report.pdf — dates sort themselves in any folder.
  • Add the document type and the person: 2026-09-14-blood-test-report-ali.pdf keeps family records apart.
  • Keep one PDF per document, with all its pages inside, rather than one file per page.
  • Back up in two places, for example your phone and a cloud folder — and keep sensitive records in a folder that is not shared with anyone.

Sharing records with a doctor

When a doctor asks for your history, send only what they asked for — not the whole folder. A single PDF per document, named by date, is easy for a clinic to open and file. If the clinic has an upload portal, check the file-size limit first and compress the PDF rather than re-scanning at lower quality. Never share medical records through a public link; send them directly to the clinic's own portal or email address.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Scanning only the front page. Lab reports and prescriptions often continue on the back — always check.
  • Blurry dosages and lab values. The numbers are the whole point of the document. Zoom in and verify them.
  • Generic file names. A folder full of "scan001.pdf" is useless in an emergency. Date-first names fix this.
  • No backup. A phone can be lost or broken. Keep a second copy somewhere safe, and keep the originals too — a scan is a copy, not a replacement for the paper record where the original is legally required.
  • Sharing the whole folder. Medical records are private. Share one document at a time, only with the doctor or clinic that asked for it.

Frequently asked questions

How do I scan medical records and prescriptions?
Scan each page in colour as a clear PDF, including both sides, and name the file with the date and document type — for example 2026-09-14-blood-test-report.pdf. Store the files in folders by person and document type, and back them up in a second place.

Should medical records be scanned in colour or black and white?
In colour. Colour preserves stamps, handwritten notes, and highlighted lab values that black and white can wash out.

Is it safe to store scanned medical records on my phone?
Yes, if the phone is locked and the files are backed up somewhere safe. Keep sensitive records in a folder that is not shared, and never share them through a public link — send them directly to the clinic that asked for them.

Do I still need the paper originals after scanning?
Keep them. A scan is a convenient copy, but some hospitals, insurers, and government offices still ask to see the original paper document.

How do I organise years of old medical paperwork?
Sort by person, then by document type, then by date — and scan the oldest or most faded documents first, while the text is still readable.
