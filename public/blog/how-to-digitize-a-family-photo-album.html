How to Digitize a Family Photo Album

By Scanner.cam Editorial Team · Updated 8 Nov 2026 · 6 min read
Quick answer

Digitize a family photo album by working through it one page at a time: photograph each page in bright, even light, crop the photos out, and save them in dated folders with names and notes. Your phone turns a shelf of albums into a searchable family archive you can share and back up.

Old albums are fragile in two ways: the photos fade, and the albums themselves fall apart. Digitizing them is a preservation project, not a race — a weekend per album is a realistic pace, and the result is a set of files your whole family can keep. The method below works whether the photos are glued to black pages, slipped into clear sleeves, or loose in a box. If you only have a handful of loose prints, start with how to scan old printed photos with your phone instead; this guide is for the whole-album project.

Plan the project before you shoot anything

  1. Count what you have. How many albums, roughly how many photos per album? A count tells you whether this is one weekend or four, and stops the project stalling halfway.
  2. Sort by year or by person. Work through one album at a time, oldest first — the oldest photos are the ones fading fastest and the ones nobody can identify any more.
  3. Gather the names now, not later. Sit with a relative who remembers, and write names, dates, and places on sticky notes before you start. Nobody will remember the people in a 1978 photo once the album is back on the shelf.
  4. Set up one station and leave it up. A table by a window, a dark cloth, and your phone. A fixed setup means every photo looks consistent and you can do an album in short sessions.
  5. Decide the folder plan first. One folder per album, named by year and event — "1985-Family-Holiday-Murree", for example. Files go straight into the right folder as you shoot.

Scan album pages — or take the photos out?

Photos in clear sleeves can usually be photographed right through the plastic, but sleeves add glare and a soft haze. If a sleeve lifts easily, take the photo out for the shot and put it straight back. Glued-down photos should stay glued: peeling them off tears the print and the page. For glued photos, photograph the whole album page, then crop each photo out of the page image afterwards — you get one clean file per photo without touching the original. Loose photos are the easiest of all: lay them flat and shoot them one by one.

Capture tips for old prints

  • Bright, even side light — never flash. Flash bounces off glossy prints and leaves a white hotspot in the middle of the photo. Window light or a desk lamp from the side is best.
  • Dark, matte background. A black cloth under the album page makes the photo edges crisp and stops the scanner confusing the page with the photo.
  • Fill the frame with the photo. The closer the shot, the more detail you keep. Check the first photo at full zoom for sharpness before doing the whole album.
  • Keep the phone parallel to the page. A tilted shot turns a rectangle into a trapezoid and loses the corners. A cheap phone tripod or a stack of books as a rest helps.
  • Photograph the back too, when it has writing. Names and dates written on the back of a print are part of the archive — shoot the back and keep it with the front file.

Label and organise the archive

A photo without a name is a mystery in ten years. Rename each file as you go: year, people, place — "1985-Ayesha-Murree.jpg" tells the story on its own. Keep the original album order inside each folder, and add a short text note per folder with anything the photos do not show: who is missing, what the occasion was, where the album is stored now. If a print is very small or you plan to enlarge it, check the best DPI for scanning old photos before you commit to a resolution. And if the album includes Polaroids, the Polaroid scanning guide covers their thick borders and glare.

Back up and share it

  1. Two copies, in two places. Keep one copy on your computer or an external drive, and one in cloud storage. A single copy on one phone is not an archive.
  2. Share the folders, not just the favourites. Send the whole album folder to the family members in the photos — they are the people most likely to keep a copy.
  3. Keep the originals. Digital copies are for sharing and viewing; the physical album is still the original. Store it flat, dry, and out of direct sunlight.
  4. Do one album, then celebrate. Finish an album completely — shoot, label, back up — before starting the next one. A shelf of half-digitized albums helps nobody.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Peeling glued photos off the page. They tear. Photograph the page and crop instead.
  • Using flash. It whites out glossy prints and hides faces.
  • Skipping the labelling. Unlabelled photos are unfindable photos. Name them while you still remember who is in them.
  • Keeping one copy on one phone. Phones get lost and break. Two copies, two places, always.
  • Trying to do every album at once. One album, finished properly, beats five albums half done.

Frequently asked questions

How do I digitize a family photo album?
Work through one album at a time: photograph each page in bright, even light, crop the photos out, name the files by year and people, and back the folders up in two places.

Should I take photos out of the album to scan them?
Take them out of clear sleeves when they lift easily. Leave glued-down photos where they are and photograph the page, then crop each photo out afterwards — peeling tears the print.

What is the best way to scan old family photos?
Flat on a dark surface, in bright side light with no flash, phone held parallel to the page and close enough to fill the frame. Check the first photo at full zoom before doing the rest.

How should I organise digitized family photos?
One folder per album, named by year and event, with files named by year, people, and place. Add a short note per folder with anything the photos do not show.

How do I share a digitized photo album with family?
Share the whole folder through cloud storage so everyone gets a complete copy — the people in the photos are the most likely to keep one. Keep your own backup as well.
