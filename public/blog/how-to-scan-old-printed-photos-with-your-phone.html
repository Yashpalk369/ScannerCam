How to Scan Old Printed Photos with Your Phone

By Scanner.cam Team · Updated 3 Nov 2026 · 4 min read
Quick answer

To scan an old printed photo with your phone, place it flat in soft, indirect daylight, hold the phone parallel above it, and capture the print without flash. Skip the flash, because it bounces off glossy prints and washes out faces; if the photo curls, weigh the edges down gently rather than pressing it flat with your fingers.

Old prints are fragile in ways documents are not: colours fade, surfaces scratch, and paper curls with age. A phone scan is a good way to preserve them, because it needs no feeder, no glass platen, and no handling beyond laying the print down. The goal is a faithful copy — the print as it looks today, colours and all — saved in a form you can share, back up, and print from again.

Prepare the print before you shoot

Handle prints by the edges, and brush off loose dust with a soft, dry cloth or a gentle puff of air — never a wet wipe, which can lift the surface. If a print is curled, lay it face down under a heavy book for a few hours before scanning, or weigh the corners down with clean, smooth objects during the shot. Do not force a brittle print flat; a cracked print is worse than a slightly curved scan.

Work on a plain, dark surface in soft daylight, such as near a window on a bright but overcast day, or a few steps back from direct sun. Direct sunlight fades prints over time and creates harsh reflections on glossy finishes; a shaded spot gives even light without either problem.

Scan one print at a time

  1. Open Scanner.cam in your phone's browser — nothing to install, and the scan is processed on your device rather than uploaded to a server.
  2. Lay one print flat, with nothing overlapping it. Scanning a pile in one frame saves time but costs resolution on every photo in it.
  3. Hold the phone parallel to the print, directly above it, with a small margin of background around all four edges. That margin helps the camera find the print's edges cleanly.
  4. Tap the print on screen to focus, wait a second for the image to sharpen, then capture. Check the result at full zoom: faces sharp, colours true, no glare band across the middle.
  5. Save each print as its own image or page, and name it while you still remember who is in it — a year, a place, or a name beats "IMG_4821" every time.

Glossy prints, matte prints, and faded colours

Glossy prints are the glare problem: if a bright band crosses the image, tilt the print slightly or move the light to the side until the reflection slides off the photo. Matte prints rarely glare but show dust more easily, so give them an extra brush first. For faded or yellowed prints, accept the colours as they are in the scan — aggressive filters can make a faded photo look stranger, not better. A faithful scan is the archive; any restoration is a separate copy made from it, never instead of it.

For very small prints, such as old wallet photos, move closer rather than zooming digitally. Digital zoom throws away detail exactly where you need it most, while a closer capture keeps the full resolution of the camera on a small subject.

What to do with the scans afterwards

Once scanned, back the files up in two places — for example, your phone plus a cloud folder or an external drive — before you put the originals away. Share copies, not originals, with family: a shared album lets everyone have the photos without the prints travelling anywhere. And keep the originals stored flat, upright in acid-free sleeves or boxes if you have them, away from attics and damp rooms, so the next generation still has something to scan.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use flash when scanning old photos?
No. Flash reflects off glossy prints and washes out faces and detail. Use soft, indirect daylight instead, and move the light to the side if you see a glare band.

Can I scan several photos in one shot?
You can, but each photo gets less resolution, and cropping them apart later is fiddly. For prints you care about, scan one at a time for the best quality.

How do I scan a curled old photo?
Lay it face down under a heavy book for a few hours first, or weigh the corners down gently during the shot. Never force a brittle print flat — it can crack.

Should I fix faded colours in the scan?
Keep the first scan faithful to the print as it is today, and treat that as your archive copy. Make any colour restoration as a separate copy, so the original scan is never lost.

What format should I save scanned photos in?
JPEG is fine for sharing and printing, and it keeps file sizes manageable. If you plan heavy editing later, save a high-quality copy too, and back both up in two places.
