How to Remove Glare When Scanning Glossy Photos

By Scanner.cam Editorial Team · Updated 9 Nov 2026 · 7 min read
Quick answer

To remove glare when scanning a glossy photo, change the angle of the light, not the camera. Lay the photo flat, move your lamp or phone so the light strikes the print from the side at roughly 30 degrees, and shoot straight down — the reflection bounces away from the lens instead of into it.

Glare is the single most common reason a scan of a glossy print looks worse than the original. The good news is that it is a lighting problem, not a camera problem, and it is fixed in seconds once you know where to put the light. This guide covers the angle trick, how to soften harsh light, and what to do when you cannot move the light at all.

Why glossy photos glare

A glossy print has a smooth, reflective coating. When light hits that coating at the same angle your camera is looking from, the reflection goes straight into the lens — and that bright spot is the glare. Matte prints scatter light in all directions, which is why they rarely glare. With a glossy print, the reflection is a mirror image of your light source, so the fix is always about geometry: change where the light comes from, and the bright spot moves out of the frame.

Two things make glare worse: a single bright light directly above the photo, and a phone held at a slight tilt. Overhead ceiling lights are the worst offenders, because they sit almost exactly where the camera looks from. That is also why scans taken at night under a room light usually glare, while the same photo scanned near a window in daylight often comes out clean.

The 30-degree rule: move the light, not the photo

Keep the photo flat and the phone directly above it. Move the light instead — that is the whole technique. Here is the setup that works in almost any room:

  1. Lay the photo flat on a dark, non-reflective surface, such as a dark cloth or a sheet of matte card. A light surface bounces extra light back up and adds haze.
  2. Turn off the overhead light if you can. One light source is far easier to control than three.
  3. Place a single lamp to the side, roughly 30 degrees from the surface of the photo — low and to one side, not above it. A desk lamp you can angle works perfectly.
  4. Hold the phone straight above the photo, parallel to it, and fill the frame. Check the screen: if you can see a bright spot, move the lamp a little further to the side until it disappears.
  5. Scan. If the photo has a foil finish or embossing, take two shots with the lamp on opposite sides and keep the cleaner one.

The angle does not have to be exact. The test is simple: if you can see the light source reflected in the print on your phone screen, move the light until you cannot. Once the reflection is gone, the scan will be clean.

Diffuse the light for a softer result

When a single lamp still leaves a hot spot, soften the light before it reaches the photo. Diffused light has no single bright point to reflect, so glare simply does not form.

  • Use window light on a cloudy day — the sky is the biggest, softest light source you will ever get for free. Place the photo near the window, not in direct sun.
  • Tape a sheet of white paper or baking parchment over a desk lamp to spread the light out. Keep the paper well away from a hot bulb.
  • Bounce the light off a white wall or ceiling instead of pointing it at the photo. The wall becomes a large, soft light source.
  • Use two lamps, one on each side, at low brightness. Two soft sources from opposite sides cancel out each other's reflections and even out the exposure.
  • Try a white bedsheet between the lamp and the photo as a quick diffuser when you are scanning a whole album in one session.

When you cannot move the light

Sometimes you are scanning at a relative's house, in an office, or somewhere the lights are fixed. You can still get a clean scan:

  • Shield the photo with your body. Stand between the overhead light and the photo so your shadow falls across it, then light it with your phone's screen or a second phone's torch held low to the side.
  • Tilt the phone very slightly — a few degrees — so the reflection moves to the edge of the print, then crop it out. Keep the tilt small or the photo will look skewed.
  • Use a polarising filter if you have one. A clip-on polariser for a phone camera cuts reflected light dramatically on glossy prints. Even holding a pair of polarised sunglasses in front of the lens and rotating them can reduce glare noticeably.
  • Scan in a darker corner of the room and use the phone's own screen as the light source, held low and to the side. It is dim, but it is a light you fully control.

Fixing glare that is already in the scan

Re-scanning with better light is almost always better than editing. Glare destroys the detail underneath it — the pixels are blown out to white, and no editor can recover information that was never recorded. If a re-scan is impossible because the original is gone, a healing or clone tool can patch a small bright spot by borrowing texture from nearby areas, but the result is a repair, not a recovery.

When you do edit, lower the highlights and raise the shadows slightly first, then check whether the glare area still draws the eye. If it does, a gentle clone from a matching area of the photo — the same sky, the same wall — will hide it better than any filter. Glossy vs matte: what changes

Matte prints are forgiving: you can scan them under almost any light, and the main risk is dull colours rather than glare. Glossy prints keep richer blacks and sharper detail, but they demand the angled-light setup in this guide. If you are reprinting an old photo, a matte finish is the safer choice for anything you plan to scan again later.

One more distinction matters: resin-coated (RC) photo paper, common in lab prints from the 1980s onwards, glares more than older fibre-based paper. If a print from that era glares no matter what you try, it is probably RC paper — diffuse the light and accept a slightly longer exposure rather than fighting the angle.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Scanning under a ceiling light. The light sits right where the camera looks from — the worst possible position.
  • Using the phone flash. The flash is next to the lens, so its reflection goes straight back into the camera.
  • Tilting the photo instead of moving the light. A tilted photo distorts the perspective and rarely moves the glare far enough.
  • Pressing the photo under glass. Glass adds a second reflective surface and usually makes glare worse, not better.
  • Accepting the first shot. Glare is visible on the phone screen before you scan — check, adjust, and only then capture.

Frequently asked questions

How do I remove glare when scanning a glossy photo?
Move the light, not the camera. Lay the photo flat, place a single lamp to the side at roughly 30 degrees, and hold the phone straight above the print — the reflection bounces away from the lens instead of into it.

Why do my phone scans of glossy photos always have a bright spot?
The bright spot is the reflection of your light source in the photo's glossy coating. Overhead lights and the phone flash sit almost exactly where the camera looks from, so their reflection goes straight into the lens.

Can I use the phone flash to scan a glossy photo?
No — the flash is the worst light for glossy prints, because it sits next to the lens and its reflection goes straight back into the camera. Turn the flash off and use a lamp placed to the side.

Does a polarising filter help with photo glare?
Yes. A clip-on polariser for a phone camera cuts reflected light from glossy prints dramatically. Even holding polarised sunglasses in front of the lens and rotating them can reduce glare noticeably.

Can I edit glare out of a scan afterwards?
Only partly. Glare blows the pixels underneath to white, so the detail is gone, not hidden. A healing or clone tool can patch a small spot, but re-scanning with angled light gives a far better result.
