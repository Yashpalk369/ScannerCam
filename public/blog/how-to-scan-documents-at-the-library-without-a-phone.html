How to Scan Documents at the Library Without a Phone

By Scanner.cam Team · Updated 30 Oct 2026 · 5 min read
Quick answer

To scan documents at the library without a phone, use the library's flatbed scanner or copier, save the scan to your own USB stick, check every page before you leave, and delete your files from the shared machine. Ask staff for help if you cannot find the scanner — most branches have one, even if it is not signposted.

Libraries are one of the most overlooked places to scan. They exist precisely for people who do not have a device, a printer, or a home office, and their scanners are usually free or cheap to use. The workflow is slightly different from phone scanning — you are working with shared equipment, so privacy and file handling need a little extra care — but the result is the same: a clean PDF you can take home.

What libraries usually offer

Most public libraries offer at least one of three things: a flatbed scanner attached to a public computer, a multifunction copier that can scan to USB or email, or a self-service kiosk. Flatbed scanners are best for delicate, bound, or single pages, because you place the page face-down and close the lid. Copiers are faster for multi-page documents, since many have an automatic feeder that scans a stack in one pass.

Availability varies by branch, and so do the rules. Some libraries require a library card to log into the computers, some limit sessions to an hour, and a few charge a small fee for printing — scanning itself is very often free. A quick question at the desk — "Where can I scan a document, and can I save it to USB?" — resolves all of this in under a minute, and staff will usually walk you to the machine.

Scan on a flatbed scanner, step by step

Log into the library computer, if needed, and open the scanning software — it is often a program supplied with the scanner, or a "Scan" option in the computer's own tools. Place the first page face-down on the glass, aligned to the corner mark, and close the lid. Choose PDF as the output format, a resolution of 300 dpi for text documents, and colour or greyscale depending on whether stamps and signatures matter.

Preview the first page before scanning the rest. A preview catches the classic mistakes: a page placed upside down, a corner hanging off the glass, or a lid left open that washes the image out. Scan each page in order, adding pages to the same document rather than creating a separate file per page, and save the finished PDF to your USB stick — not to the computer's desktop, where the next user could find it.

Scan on a copier, step by step

On a multifunction copier, look for a "Scan" or "Send" button on the touchscreen, then choose USB as the destination and plug in your stick. For loose pages, use the feeder on top: stack the pages face-up in order, and the machine pulls them through one by one. For bound books or ID cards, use the flat glass instead, placing each page face-down.

Choose PDF and, if offered, a "multi-page" or "single file" option so the whole document lands in one file. Scan, wait for the confirmation, and open the file on the library computer or on your own device to check the page order before you walk away. Copier feeders occasionally pull two pages at once, and a missing middle page is much harder to fix from home.

Save to USB, not to email, when you can

A USB stick is the best destination for library scans, for two reasons. First, it keeps your document off other people's servers: emailing a scan to yourself creates a copy in your inbox and possibly on the copier's sent log. Second, it avoids the common failure where a mistyped email address sends your document to a stranger.

If you do not have a USB stick, emailing the scan to yourself is a workable fallback — double-check the address character by character before sending, and delete the sent copy from the copier's history if the machine offers that option. Some libraries also let you save to a cloud account you log into on the computer; if you do that, remember to log out fully afterwards.

Clear your traces before you leave

Shared machines remember things, so build a two-minute cleanup into every library visit. Delete any scan files saved on the computer or the copier, remove your USB stick, log out of the computer and any accounts you opened, and clear the copier's recent-jobs list if it shows your file. This matters most for personal documents: a scan left on a public computer is effectively a scan left in a public place.

The same rule applies in reverse — if the library computer has a webcam and a browser, you can also scan there with a browser-based scanner, which processes pages on that machine and lets you download the PDF straight to your USB stick without creating a cloud copy. Just remember the cleanup step either way.

Frequently asked questions

Is scanning at the library free?
Very often, yes — most public libraries offer scanning free of charge, though some charge for printing. Policies vary by branch, so ask at the desk if you are unsure.

Do I need a library card to scan?
Sometimes. You may need a card to log into a public computer, but copier scanning to USB often needs no login at all. Bring your card if you have one, and ask staff if you do not.

Can I scan a bound book or a thick document?
Yes, on the flatbed glass, one page at a time. Press the book gently flat and close the lid as far as it will go; avoid forcing a thick spine, which can damage the binding and blur the page near it.

How do I keep my scans private on a shared computer?
Save to your own USB stick, delete any copies from the computer and copier, log out of everything, and take the stick with you. Never leave the only copy of a sensitive scan on a public machine.

What if my library has no scanner?
Ask staff — many branches keep a scanner in a back office or at a specific branch nearby. Otherwise, a library computer with a webcam and a browser scanner, or a friend's phone for a few minutes, will cover most documents.
